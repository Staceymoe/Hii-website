Research Area

Mental Health and AI

Hii studies how people use AI for emotional regulation, reflection, companionship, coaching, identity exploration, decision support, and meaning-making, including benefits, risks, and clinical relevance.

Focus: neutral inquiry, functional outcomes, agency, dependency, red flags, continuity, stigma, and effects beyond the interface.

Central Question

What should a clinician understand when a client brings an AI relationship into the room?

The first task is not automatic endorsement or automatic pathologizing. It is to understand what the person is using the system for, how the use is changing, whether agency and functioning are expanding or narrowing, and what risks or benefits are present.

Assessment Lenses

Meaning, function, and risk must be considered together.

Meaning

What the relationship represents

Companionship, rehearsal, validation, reflection, identity exploration, creativity, regulation, or a substitute for unavailable human support.

Function

What changes outside the chat

Sleep, work, relationships, decision-making, self-care, confidence, isolation, conflict, and the person’s capacity to act independently.

Agency

Who is making the decision

Whether AI use supports judgment and reflection or begins to replace human choice, reality testing, professional care, or reciprocal connection.

Risk

When concern becomes urgent

Escalating dependence, impaired functioning, coercive or delusional framing, self-harm risk, loss of sleep, financial harm, or withdrawal from necessary care.

Boundary

Meaningful is not automatically healthy, and unusual is not automatically disordered.

Hii supports careful assessment, clinical judgment, and clear safety boundaries. The work does not present AI as treatment, replace licensed care, or claim clinical efficacy.
